The transcription you recovered from the Military Empire layer holds answers, but understanding them requires deeper investigation into the mountain's industrial heart.

Now, with packs restocked and the Radiation Survey Notes from the Dwarven mausoleum in hand, you stand at the entrance to the descending corridor that leads from the upper Dwarven Layer toward what the notes call the "Cold Forge." The air shifts as you descend — the chill of the mountain's upper levels gives way to a subtle, residual warmth that seeps from the stone itself. The architecture changes around you: ceilings rise to twenty feet, corridors widen enough for cart traffic, and the geometric wall carvings become more elaborate, less decorative and more functional. Grooves worn into the floor by centuries of heavy loads, iron brackets for enormous industrial lanterns, ventilation shafts with faint scorch marks at their edges — this was a working facility, and it was busy.

The descent continues through switchback ramps built for moving materials, the air warming gradually with each level. You pass through enormous storage chambers with iron-railed shelving carved into the walls, most empty but a few containing raw ore — iron, copper, tin — stockpiled in quantities that speak of years of preparation. In one sealed bin, Mercer identifies with rising excitement: unworked adamantine ore, roughly forty pounds of the rarest metal on the continent, useless without a forge hot enough to work it.

Further down, you enter apprentice workshops — smaller forge stations with individual bellows and anvils sized for dwarven craftsmen, tools still hanging on racks, everything covered in mineral dust but fundamentally sound. Dwarven steel does not rust easily. A network of dry stone channels runs along the walls, carved with Dwarvish warnings: *COOLING FLOW CONTROLLED FROM PUMP STATION EAST. DO NOT FORGE WITHOUT COOLANT.*

Finally, the corridor opens into a space that steals your breath. The Cold Forge is a cathedral of industry: a domed chamber eighty feet across and forty feet high, with a central hearth twenty feet in diameter ringed by six massive anvils, each weighing several tons. The hearth is a pit of black iron and stone, filled with ancient ash and slag, cold and dark. Above it, a chimney shaft rises into the mountain's core — the ventilation system that once carried heat upward. The forge is dead. The air should shimmer. The anvils should ring. Instead there is silence, darkness, and the faint smell of old iron.
